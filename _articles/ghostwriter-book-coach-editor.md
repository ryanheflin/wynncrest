The simplest distinction is this:

- A **ghostwriter** helps create the manuscript.
- A **book coach** helps the author create the manuscript.
- An **editor** improves a manuscript that already exists.

In practice, the lines can overlap, but understanding those three jobs makes it much easier to choose the right help.

## When a ghostwriter makes sense

A ghostwriter is usually the right fit when the author has the ideas, experience, stories, expertise, or source material but does not have the time or desire to draft the entire book.

A professional [book ghostwriting](/ghostwriting/) process often includes:

- concept and structure development;
- interviews with the author;
- review of source materials;
- additional research;
- chapter drafting;
- author feedback;
- revision.

The author remains responsible for the ideas, factual accuracy, point of view, and final approval. The ghostwriter supplies much of the writing labor and manuscript craft.

## When a book coach makes sense

A book coach is more appropriate when **you want to do the writing yourself** but benefit from structure, deadlines, feedback, accountability, and guidance.

A coach may help you:

- clarify the concept;
- build an outline;
- establish a writing schedule;
- troubleshoot chapters;
- identify recurring problems;
- stay accountable.

The result is still primarily your draft.

## When you need a developmental editor

A developmental editor usually enters after substantial material exists.

Developmental editing looks at the big questions:

- Does the structure work?
- Is the argument clear?
- Are chapters doing distinct jobs?
- Is important material missing?
- Is the pacing effective?
- Are sections repetitive?
- Does the book deliver on its promise?

This can involve significant restructuring or rewriting.

## Copyediting and proofreading come later

Copyediting focuses more closely on clarity, grammar, style, consistency, usage, and presentation.

Proofreading happens much closer to publication, usually after the book has been typeset or otherwise designed. It catches typographical errors and production-stage mistakes.

These are different stages. A proofreader should not be expected to fix a book whose structure is still unsettled.

## Many projects need more than one kind of help

A founder might begin with a development strategist, move into ghostwriting, then work with developmental and copy editors before [design and production](/editing-production/).

Another author may write independently, hire a developmental editor, revise, and then move into production.

The important question is not “Which professional should every author hire?”

It is:

> **What does this particular book need next?**

A good publishing process diagnoses that before assigning services.
